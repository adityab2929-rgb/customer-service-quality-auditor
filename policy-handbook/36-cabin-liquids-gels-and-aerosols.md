# 36 — Cabin liquids, gels, and aerosols

## Scope and customer language

Retrieve for “100 ml,” “toiletries,” “shampoo,” “toothpaste,” “liquid bag,” “duty-free at a connection,” or “they took my bottle.” The airport security authority sets the screening limit. It can be stricter than the airline’s wish.

## Required action

Unless the departure airport publishes a different current limit, each liquid, gel, cream, paste, or aerosol in cabin baggage must be in a container of 100 ml or less, and all of those containers must fit in one transparent resealable bag of about one litre, one bag per passenger. Containers larger than 100 ml are refused in the cabin even when partly empty. Medication, baby food, and baby milk may be exempt only when the security officer accepts them after inspection; tell the passenger to declare those items at screening, not to hide them in the liquid bag.

If a container is over the limit, the passenger may move it to checked baggage when the item is lawful there, decant it into a 100 ml container before security, surrender it, or leave it with someone not travelling. A sealed duty-free bag is accepted on a connection only when that airport’s transfer rule and the bag’s seal and receipt allow it. Surrendered items are not refunded by the airline. Do not suggest ways to disguise a liquid.

## Escalation and examples

Escalate a medical liquid that screening refused, a duty-free bag opened on a connection, or a passenger who is about to miss the flight because of repacking.

**Compliant:** “A 200 ml bottle cannot go through the cabin even if it is half full. You can put it in checked baggage if that product is allowed there, or leave it here.”

**Potentially non-compliant:** “The liquid rule is over, so any size bottle is fine.”

**Authority:** departure-airport security rules; [TSA liquids rule](https://www.tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/liquids-aerosols-and-gels).
