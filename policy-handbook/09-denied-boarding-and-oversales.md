# 09 — Denied boarding, oversales, and downgrades

## Scope and fact check

Retrieve for “bumped,” “oversold,” “no seat,” “standby,” “volunteer,” or “downgraded.” Establish whether the passenger volunteered, arrived for check-in/boarding on time with required documents, held a confirmed reservation, and was denied for oversales, operational, safety, documentation, conduct, or another reason. These distinctions are material.

## Required action

Never call a customer an involuntary denial of boarding before the airport/operations record confirms it. Where oversales is anticipated, the carrier should first seek volunteers under the applicable process; a volunteer arrangement must be explained and recorded. If denied boarding involuntarily, provide the mandated written rights notice and immediate assistance/compensation workflow applicable to the itinerary, not an unsupported promise. Refund/adjust unused optional services if required where they were not received.

EU261 and US DOT rules can apply to certain involuntary denied-boardings, but eligibility and amount depend on exact itinerary and cause. A denial caused by missing travel documents, safety, health/security requirements, late arrival, or a passenger’s conduct may be treated differently. Cabin downgrade may have separate rights; collect original and flown cabin, price, and reason.

## Escalation and examples

Escalate a dispute about volunteering, documents, oversales reason, compensation calculation, accessibility needs, or a police/security incident. Do not tell a customer to buy another ticket to preserve rights without approval.

**Compliant:** “I will verify whether this was an involuntary oversales denial and provide the applicable written notice and review process.”

**Potentially non-compliant:** “Anyone without a seat gets the same payout.”

**Authority:** [US DOT Fly Rights](https://www.transportation.gov/airconsumer/fly-rights), [EU Regulation 261/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2004/261/oj/eng).
